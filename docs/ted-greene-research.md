## The Honest Summary

This is a private archive of roughly 400+ PDFs spanning 35 years of teaching notes (1967–2005), organized by topic but never by learning sequence. Greene clearly filed as he went. The categories are real, the content is extraordinary, but the pedagogy was *him* — you got the sequence when you sat across from him.

## What's Actually There

### Baroque (~110 PDFs)

The motherlode for the baroque improv goal. Two sub-groups:

**Main lessons** cover:
- Counterpoint types: 1-to-1, 2-to-1, 2- and 3-part, Mozartean, Modern
- Bass harmonization and bass movement patterns
- Cycle-of-4ths progressions (major and minor)
- Modulation schemes and secondary chord "openers"
- Cadences and cadential bass formulas
- Pedal points (Bach-type, inner pedals, soprano pedals)
- Voice leading: 6ths with inner pedals, 10ths, contrary motion, diatonic 6ths
- Chorale style phrases, first and second inversions
- Neo-Baroque variations (ascending/descending bass progressions, light colors)

**"Baroque Ideas" #1–#14**: shorter, focused etudes — the practical application layer on top of the theory docs.

Key documents explicitly about improvisation:
- *"Typical Key Schemes and General Plans for Baroque Improvisation"* (1975)
- *"Indicative Examples of Baroque — For Improvisation and Composing"* (1980)
- *"Baroque Practice Program"* (1975) — hints at an actual study sequence

Dates span ~1970–1995, with most from 1974–1985. Greene was building this vocabulary over two decades.

### The V-System (~40 PDFs + 11 sub-pages)

The most organized section on the entire site — the one exception to the "beautiful chaos" rule.

**Core concept**: Greene classified all four-note guitar chords into 14 "voicing groups" (V-1 through V-14) based on how the chord tones are spaced relative to each other. Once you know a chord's voicing group, you can navigate it systematically across the entire neck. This is the underlying architecture for all of his more advanced harmonic work.

Three distinct layers:

**1. James Hober's structured explication** (29 sequential PDFs):
- Welcome + Who Is James
- V-System Introduction
- Method 1: How to Recognize (+ For the Table-Challenged, By Letter Name, Quiz #1)
- The Natural String Sets
- Method 1: How to Build (+ Quiz #2)
- Method 2: The Chord Tone Gap Method (+ Further Insights, Quizzes #3 and #4)
- The 43 Four-Note Qualities
- The Mathematics of Four-Note Chords and Beyond
- Method 3 (+ Computer Completion variants, Algorithm)
- Conversions (4 documents: by procedure, source group, target group)
- The Fixed Soprano Tour + The Fixed Bass Tour
- About the Rosetta Page
- How Systematic Inversions Relate to the V-System
- Ted's Plans for Teaching the V-System
- Acknowledgments

This is a complete, sequential curriculum written *about* Greene's system. Hober did the sequencing work that Greene never formalized. It appears to be typeset (not handwritten), making it cleanly parseable.

**2. Ted's original handwritten notes** (~10 PDFs):
- The "Rosetta Page" (1989) — his master cross-reference
- Chord Tone Gap Method (his version)
- Master Sheet on All 4-Note Chord Types (1980)
- V-System Conversion Methods (1989, 2003)
- Lesson transcripts with specific students (Mark Levy 1992, Kevin Griffin 1989, Paul Vachon 1985)
- Reflections on teaching V-2 chord groups

**3. V-1 through V-14 lesson sheets**: each is a separate sub-page. V-1 through V-10 have individual pages; V-11 through V-14 are combined; plus a "Combined groups" page. Each sub-page likely contains many individual lesson sheets — this is probably the largest portion of the V-System content by volume.

Dates from 1967 to 2005. Alphabetically listed, zero inherent sequence. Would need to be organized around a theory curriculum framework.

### Single-Note Soloing (~80 PDFs)

Less directly relevant to the baroque improv goal, but the arpeggio and melodic pattern content overlaps with counterpoint application.

## What This Means for Baroque Improv Goals

**The baroque improv path is real and Greene was explicit about it.** He wasn't teaching classical guitar performance — he was teaching baroque as a *grammar for improvisation*. The comparison to jazz is directly apt. Just as a jazz musician internalizes ii-V-I and its substitutions deeply enough to play in the moment, Greene wanted students to internalize baroque harmonic tendencies, bass movement patterns, and counterpoint rules deeply enough to *think* in them in real time.

**The V-System is the structural backbone.** The baroque vocabulary is essentially a collection of chord voicings + voice-leading moves. The V-System is the filing system that makes those voicings *findable* under your fingers anywhere on the neck. They're not separate courses — baroque improv is what the V-System is *for*. You build the voicing navigation first, then the baroque harmonic grammar gives you the map for where to go.

**The DADGAD connection is implicit, not explicit.** None of Greene's material is in DADGAD — he worked in standard tuning. But the principles (voice leading, bass movement, pedal tones, cycle progressions) are tuning-agnostic. The voicing groups (V-1 through V-14) are defined by interval relationships between chord tones, not by specific string positions. This means the V-System *can* be remapped to DADGAD intervals — it's real work, but it's well-defined work. In DADGAD, certain voicing groups become easier (open string resonance, dropped bass notes) and others harder. That remapping is actually a strong AMF-specific contribution that doesn't exist anywhere else.

**The handwritten content is a real challenge, but survivable.** Greene's handwriting is unusually consistent and readable for a musician's notes — far from the worst-case scenario. The bigger challenge isn't legibility — it's that most content uses fretboard diagrams rather than standard notation, which means digital interpretation requires guitar-specific spatial reasoning (string, fret, finger → pitch → harmonic role), not just OCR. The Hober V-System explication appears typeset and would be cleanly parseable. The "Baroque Ideas" etudes are probably the most diagram-heavy.

## The Hidden Organizing Principle

Greene had an implied progression that can be reconstructed from context, even though he never wrote it down as a curriculum:

| Stage | Content | Notes |
|-------|---------|-------|
| 1 | **Fundamentals** | Basic chord theory, reading fretboard diagrams |
| 2 | **Chord Studies** | Four-note chord vocabulary — the raw material |
| 3 | **V-System V-1 through ~V-6** | Lighter voicing groups, closer to standard chord shapes |
| 4 | **Harmony & Theory (core)** | Progressions and their substitutions |
| 5 | **V-System V-7 through V-14** | Wider, more complex voicings |
| 6 | **Baroque (counterpoint foundation)** | 1-to-1, 2-to-1 counterpoint; bass harmonization; cycle progressions |
| 7 | **Baroque (harmonic grammar)** | Modulation schemes; key vocabulary; characteristic progressions |
| 8 | **Baroque (improvisation application)** | "Typical Key Schemes," "Indicative Examples," Baroque Practice Program |
| 9 | **Baroque Ideas #1–#14** | The etudes where the grammar becomes music |

The "From Students" section and the newsletter/forum archives on the site contain hints about how Greene actually paced this with real students — that would be worth mining for sequence clues before building the organization system.

## Recommendations for Phase 2 (Downloading & Organizing)

When ready to download and organize:

**Start with the V-System** — it's already sequenced by Hober, cleaner to parse (typeset content), and is the structural skeleton everything else hangs on. The Hober explication documents should be Stage 1 of any organized system.

**Anchor the Baroque section** around "Baroque Practice Program" as the organizing document, then sort others by implied prerequisite:
1. Counterpoint types (1-to-1 → 2-to-1 → 3-part)
2. Bass harmonization and movement patterns  
3. Characteristic progressions (cycle of 4ths, diatonic progressions, inversions)
4. Modulation schemes
5. Improvisation application documents
6. Baroque Ideas #1–#14 (applied etudes)

**Mine the From Students section** for teaching sequence evidence — these are lesson transcripts that would show how Greene actually moved students through material.

**Download the YouTube channel index** (TedGreeneArchives) — some lessons have associated videos that make the handwritten diagrams readable. PDFs with the "new video" icon on the site are tagged to specific videos.

**Leave Jazz/Blues/Comping** for a second pass — valuable but not the primary goal.

## AMF Integration Angle

The V-System maps cleanly to a voicing library — V-1 through V-14 as navigable chord groups with fingering positions. The Baroque section maps to a "harmonic patterns" module — progressions the user can audition and transpose. The YouTube channel (which is active and well-maintained) gives playable examples to anchor each concept aurally.

Most importantly: a DADGAD remapping of the V-System voicing groups doesn't exist anywhere in the world right now. If AMF could generate V-System equivalents for DADGAD — showing which voicing groups gain new possibilities under the open tuning, which require different fingering strategies, and which are unchanged — that would be a genuinely novel contribution to the intersection of baroque guitar study and alternate tuning work.

The combination of Greene's framework + AMF's arranger/notation rendering + DADGAD translation is the project.
