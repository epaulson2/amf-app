Chapter 7: Interval Spelling at the Keyboard

An interval is the distance between two notes. In Ch.7 you learn to identify and spell any interval using only the keyboard layout — no formulas, no counting half-steps mechanically.

The Pythagorean Method (Review from Ch.6)

Arrange the seven note names in this order: F — C — G — D — A — E — B

  • Left = flat side (F is the flattest). Right = sharp side (B is the sharpest).
  • Adjacent letters in this chain share six of seven scale notes — they are maximally close.
  • To spell an interval: find both notes on the chain. Count the steps between them. The count tells you the interval quality.
  • One step apart = perfect fifth (or fourth, depending on direction). Two steps apart = major second. Three steps = major sixth.
Keyboard anchor rule: The two-black-key group = C (left), D (middle), E (right). The three-black-key group = F (left), G, A (middle two), B (right). Knowing this by sight lets you place any note instantly.

Sprint 4 Interval Targets

By end of sprint, identify any interval on the keyboard using the Pythagorean chain — no counting half-steps one by one.

S1 — Discovering
Hear before you play. Sing the interval "F up to C" (perfect 5th). Then find F and C on the keyboard. Notice they are one step apart on the Pythagorean chain (F–C). Repeat with G–D, C–G. These are all P5ths. Recognize the sound before the name.
S2 — Perfecting
Hear before you play. Pick any two white keys at random. Before naming the interval, sing the distance first. Then use the Pythagorean chain to confirm. Practice: Eb to Bb (P5), Eb to Ab (P4), Eb to C (maj6 = raised 6 of Dorian!).
S3 — Fluent
Hear before you play. Teacher names any two notes. You name the interval in under 2 seconds, using only the chain. Include enharmonic pairs: Db = C# (one step to left of D on chain). Target: all diatonic intervals in Eb Dorian fluently.

Chapter 9: Di-Chord Numbers — Introduction

Traditional interval names (minor 3rd, perfect 5th) were designed for written notation. Di-Chord Numbers are designed for hearing and playing. They give each interval a number [1]–[11] that describes its emotional character directly.

Why Di-Chord Numbers Matter for Superstition

The Eb7 chord in Superstition contains two di-chord intervals that define its sound:

  • Eb to Db = a [10] minor 7th. This is the "blues lean" — tense, wanting to resolve down.
  • Eb to C = a [9] major 6th. This is the Dorian raised-6 — open, surprisingly bright for a minor chord.
  • Eb to Bb = a [7] perfect 5th. Stable, the harmonic foundation.
  • These three intervals stacked = the emotional color of the entire song groove.

Chamber 1 Melody Chamber — Duration Amplifies Zone

Duration Amplifies Zone

You already know the four zones: Zone 1 (Sweet: 1, 3, 5), Zone 2 (Bitter: 7, 2), Zone 3 (Tense: 4), Zone 4 (Wildcard: 6). This sprint reveals a critical hidden dimension: how long you hold a note changes how much tension it creates.

The Duration Rule

Long note in Z1 (Sweet): Deep, settled resolution. Feels like coming home and staying. The longer you hold 1 or 5, the more grounded the phrase.

Short note in Z1: Passes through — sweet but not anchored. A quick 1 doesn’t land with the same weight.

Long note in Z3 (Tense: 4): Much more tension. A held 4 (Ab in Eb Dorian) insists on resolution. It doesn’t let the listener relax.

Short note in Z3: Passes through lightly. A quick 4 creates movement without demanding resolution.

Zone 3 in Eb Dorian Context

In Eb Dorian, the scale degree 4 = Ab. When Stevie Wonder’s vocal line lands on Ab and holds it over an Eb7 chord, you feel the pull immediately. The longer it stays, the more the ear screams for resolution.

Dorian 4 (Ab over Eb7): This note is NOT in the Eb7 chord (Eb, Gb, Bb, Db). It sits a half step above the chord’s 3rd (Gb) and a whole step below the 5th (Bb). Held long, it creates a suspended-fourth feeling. When it resolves down to Gb or up to Bb, the release is physical.

The Dorian Raised-6 (Zone 4 Wildcard)

Dorian’s defining note is its natural 6 — C natural (not Cb as in natural minor / Aeolian). In Eb Dorian this is C. Its zone status depends entirely on context:

Over Eb7 (tonic)C (natural 6)Z4 WildcardBright, jazzy, unexpected warmth
Over Ab7 (IV chord)C (maj3rd of Ab)Z1 SweetFully resolved, inside the chord

Progression #4: ii-V-I — Drill Vehicle, Not Harmonic Spine

Critical framing: ii-V-I is a drill vehicle — a useful exercise for practicing voice leading and jazz vocabulary. It is one of fourteen progressions in the AMF system, not the foundation of music. Real songs use it as a moment, not as their entire structure.

In Eb major the ii-V-I is: Fm7 → Bb7 → Ebmaj7. In C major: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7. In G major: Am7 → D7 → Gmaj7.

Why ii-V-I Works (Mechanically)

  • The ii chord (Fm7 in Eb) introduces the 4th scale degree (Ab) — a Zone 3 note that creates tension.
  • The V7 chord (Bb7) contains the tritone [6] between its 3rd (D) and 7th (Ab). Tritone = frozen, must resolve.
  • The I chord (Ebmaj7) resolves both tensions simultaneously. The tritone dissolves. Zone 3 resolves to Zone 1.
  • Result: the motion from ii to V7 to I is the most efficient tension-and-release mechanism in tonal harmony.

Modal Vamp vs. Functional Harmony

Superstition does not use ii-V-I as its harmonic engine. It uses a modal vamp — a groove that establishes a tonal center (Eb) without needing to "resolve" to it. The chord moves (Eb7→Ab7→Bb7) create motion and color, but there is no cadence, no resolution point. The tonic never goes away.

Eb Dorian Modal Vamp — Full Breakdown

Chord Structure

The harmony in Superstition is built on a single modal center: Eb Dorian. The primary progression circles through three dominant-7th chords:

Eb7
Tonic chord
Eb-Gb-Bb-Db
4 bars (verse)
Ab7
IV chord
Ab-C-Eb-Gb
Color / lift
Bb7
V chord
Bb-D-F-Ab
Maximum tension

Full progression (12-bar-adjacent, but modal):

Eb7 | Eb7 | Eb7 | Eb7
Ab7 | Ab7 | Eb7 | Eb7
Bb7 | Ab7 | Eb7 | (turnaround)
Why dominant 7ths on every chord? In Dorian, the tonic chord naturally carries a minor 7th (Db in Eb7). When this same quality extends to IV (Ab7) and V (Bb7), the music stays in one modal world without any chord functioning as a "dominant that resolves." Every chord is equally tense and equally at home. This is the Dorian paradox.

Eb Dorian Explained

Dorian Scale: Eb-F-Gb-Ab-Bb-C-Db

vs. Eb Natural Minor (Aeolian): Eb-F-Gb-Ab-Bb-Cb-Db

Dorian raises the 6th degree: Cb → C natural. That single change creates the "jazzy minor" color.

The raised 6 (C natural) creates a major 6th above Eb — di-chord [9]. Bright, unexpected. It’s why Dorian sounds simultaneously minor and hopeful.

Mode brightness ranking (Pythagorean / Ch.6): Lydian > Ionian > Mixolydian > Dorian > Aeolian > Phrygian > Locrian. Dorian sits exactly in the middle — the most balanced minor mode.

What to Listen For

The Clav Groove

The clavinet IS the rhythm section. Listen for the anticipations — notes that hit on the "and" of 4 (position 8 of the grid) rather than beat 1. This is what makes the groove feel like it’s always leaning forward.

The Flat-7 (Db)

The Db in the Eb7 chord is your Zone 2 blues note. When the melody hits Db and holds it, you hear the characteristic "lean" of blues-funk. It’s inside the chord but never feels fully resolved.

IV chord Arrival

When Eb7 moves to Ab7, the C natural (Dorian raised-6) suddenly becomes the 3rd of Ab7 — fully inside the chord. Zone 4 Wildcard becomes Zone 1 Sweet. Chord changes transform a note’s meaning.

Son Clave: 2-3 Orientation

In Sprint 3 you learned the 3-2 clave — the three-side comes first. Sprint 4 adds the 2-3 orientation, which flips the two measures. Same five notes, different starting point, completely different groove feel.

Orientation matters: 3-2 clave front-loads the three-side and feels like it’s pushing forward. 2-3 clave holds back in measure one and then surges in measure two. Superstition’s clav pattern lives closer to the 2-3 feel — the first beat of the pattern is sparse, and the density builds into the second measure.

Groove Development: 4-Step Process

1
Boring: Play the chord or bass note on every beat, evenly. Pure quarter notes, no swing. This establishes the harmonic rhythm before adding any groove. Play Eb7 quarter notes for 2 minutes at 100 BPM.
2
Anticipations: Move the first note of the second measure to the "and of 4" in the first measure (position 8). Instead of landing on beat 1, you arrive early. This single change creates forward motion. Add one anticipation at a time.
3
Stops: The last note before a rest is rhythmically the most important note. Add a “stop” — a note that hits and then is deliberately silenced. The silence after the stop creates space, and the silence is where the groove breathes.
4
Clave Sync: Layer your groove against the 2-3 clave pattern. Your strongest hits should complement (not duplicate) the clave beats. Where the clave is silent, your groove can fill. Where the clave hits, your groove confirms or creates space.

Superstition Clav Pattern Study

The clavinet riff in Superstition is one of the most analyzed grooves in funk history. Its key rhythmic features:

  • Strong offbeat feel — many attacks land on the "ands," not on the downbeats
  • 16th-note subdivisions — the riff lives in the space between beats
  • Call-and-response structure — the riff asks a question; the rest answers
  • Minimal left hand — the left hand provides the Eb7 chord foundation while the right hand riffs
  • Damped articulation — most notes are short (staccato), not held
  • Three chord centers: riff on Eb, lift to Ab, tension to Bb
S1 — Discovering
Hear before you play. Listen to the intro of Superstition (0:00–0:08, just the clav). Clap along with only the downbeats (1, 2, 3, 4). Notice how the clav avoids landing directly on your clap. That gap between your clap and the clav attack IS the groove.
S2 — Perfecting
Hear before you play. Tap the clav rhythm on your lap using the Lap Map (Ch.5). Count the 8-position grid out loud while tapping. Identify which positions are active. Then transfer to your instrument.
S3 — Fluent
Hear before you play. Play the verse groove (simplified version: Eb7 with anticipation on “and of 4”) at 80 BPM without stopping for 2 minutes. Add one stop. Add one anticipation. Keep the groove circular — it should feel like it loops indefinitely.

Chamber 4 Voicings Chamber — Triad Shapes by Stringset

Guitar: All Inversions on All 4 String Sets

Sprint 4 primary voicing content: triads on every string set, in all three inversions. This is the foundational voicing vocabulary for guitar — Beato pp. 93–100.

Why String Sets Matter

A string set is any group of three adjacent strings. On a standard-tuned guitar there are four three-string sets: strings 6-5-4, strings 5-4-3, strings 4-3-2, and strings 3-2-1. Learning the same triad on all four string sets means you can play any harmony anywhere on the neck without shifting positions.

Each string set has a different voicing spread and register character: the 6-5-4 set sounds thick and low; the 3-2-1 set sounds bright and singing.

String Set Shapes — Eb Minor Triad (Eb-Gb-Bb)

Fret positions are illustrative for one voicing per set; multiple positions exist across the neck.

Strings 6-5-4 (Low Set)

Thick, dark voicing. Root position has Eb on string 6 (11th fret). 1st inversion: Gb on string 6 (2nd fret). 2nd inversion: Bb on string 6 (6th fret). Use for rhythm guitar foundation — locks with bass.

Strings 5-4-3 (Mid-Low Set)

Warm, full. Root position: Eb on string 5 (6th fret). 1st inversion: Gb on string 5 (9th fret). 2nd inversion: Bb on string 5 (1st fret). Works well for chord melody and chord-soloing.

Strings 4-3-2 (Mid-High Set)

Clear, mid-range. Root position: Eb on string 4 (1st fret). 1st inversion: Gb on string 4 (4th fret). 2nd inversion: Bb on string 4 (8th fret). Essential for fills and melodic comping.

Strings 3-2-1 (High Set)

Bright, singing, cuts through. Root position: Eb on string 3 (8th fret). 1st inversion: Gb on string 3 (11th fret). 2nd inversion: Bb on string 3 (3rd fret). Use for high lead parts and arpeggiated passages.

S1 — Discovering
Hear before you play. Pick one string set (start with 3-2-1). Play the three Eb minor inversions slowly, one after another. Before playing each shape, sing the three notes (Eb, Gb, Bb in some order) out loud. Notice how the same three notes sound different depending on which note is on the bottom.
S2 — Perfecting
Hear before you play. On one string set, cycle root → 1st → 2nd → root at 60 BPM. Then do the same for Ab minor and Bb minor. Target: all three inversions on two string sets at 80 BPM.

Piano: Dorian Heptachord + Shell Voicing

Eb Dorian Heptachord

All 7 notes stacked: Eb-F-Gb-Ab-Bb-C-Db. LH: Eb-Gb-Bb (triad). RH: Db-Eb-F (top cluster). Play as arpeggios bottom to top. Listen for C natural (raised 6) floating above the minor texture.

Shell Voicing Drill

Shell = root + 3rd + 7th (omit 5th). Eb7: Eb+Gb+Db · Ab7: Ab+C+Gb · Bb7: Bb+D+Ab. Note: Ab7's 3rd is C natural — the Dorian raised-6!
